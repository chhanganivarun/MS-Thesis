\section{Introduction to Autonomous Navigation}

Autonomous navigation is the science of enabling a robot or a vehicle to move through its environment without continuous human control~\cite{Thrun2005}. It is fundamental to self-driving cars, delivery robots, and related systems~\cite{Siegwart2011, Siciliano2016}. The classical pipeline comprises several interconnected stages: perception, localization, mapping, path planning, and control~\cite{Latombe1991}.

For road vehicles, the importance of autonomous navigation can be summarised as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Safety and efficiency:} Reducing human error while improving traffic flow and mobility~\cite{Toghi2021}.
\item \textbf{Real-time operation:} Perception and planning must run at rates compatible with driving dynamics~\cite{Dominguez2015}.
\item \textbf{Coexistence with humans:} Agents must interact safely with human-driven vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists in mixed traffic~\cite{Toghi2021}.
\item \textbf{Open research problems:} Dynamic scenes, language-conditioned goals, and continuous control remain active research areas that motivate the work in this thesis.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Perception} uses sensors such as cameras, LiDAR, radar, and ultrasonics to observe the environment~\cite{Borenstein1996,Siegwart2011}.
\textbf{Localization} estimates the vehicle's pose within that environment~\cite{Thrun2005}, using odometry, inertial measurement units (IMUs), GPS, or Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)~\cite{DurrantWhyte2006}.
\textbf{Mapping} builds a representation of the surroundings---from a simple 2D occupancy grid to a rich 3D map---for planning and obstacle avoidance~\cite{Thrun2002,Elfes1989}.
\textbf{Path planning} computes a route from the current pose to a goal while accounting for obstacles~\cite{LaValle2006,Kavraki1996}.
\textbf{Control} executes the plan by issuing actuator commands and correcting deviations from feedback~\cite{Siciliano2010}.

Common navigation paradigms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reactive navigation:} real-time responses without a global map~\cite{Arkin1998,Braitenberg1986}; fast in dynamic scenes, but not always globally efficient.
    \item \textbf{Map-based navigation:} planning on a known map~\cite{Thrun2005,Elfes1989}; efficient when the map is accurate, brittle when the world changes.
    \item \textbf{SLAM:} jointly estimating pose and map in an unknown environment~\cite{DurrantWhyte2006,Thrun2004,Leonard1991}, with LiDAR-based and visual variants~\cite{Zhang2014}.
\end{itemize}

In this thesis, the local planner plays the role of path planning and control, while our contribution focuses on perception and language understanding: predicting which road region the vehicle should drive toward given a linguistic command (Figure~\ref{fig:vln_setup_schematic}).

\section{Basics of Computer Vision}

Computer vision develops methods for acquiring, processing, and interpreting digital images so that machines can extract information useful for decisions~\cite{Szeliski2010}. Image data may come from single cameras, multi-camera rigs, RGB-D sensors, or LiDAR. Sub-domains include object detection, semantic segmentation, tracking, reconstruction, and related tasks. In autonomous driving, vision is used to detect lanes, vehicles, pedestrians, and traffic signals, and to understand the road scene~\cite{Chen2015}.

\subsection{Basic Methods in Computer Vision}

Methods especially relevant to language-guided driving include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Object Detection:} localising specific objects such as vehicles, pedestrians, and signs~\cite{redmon2016you,Ren2017Faster}.
    \item \textbf{Image Segmentation:} partitioning an image into regions that are easier to analyse~\cite{Chinki2012kmeans}, e.g.\ road versus sidewalk~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Segmentation:} assigning a class label to every pixel, which is useful for identifying drivable surface, pedestrians, and vehicles~\cite{badrinarayanan2017segnet}.
\end{itemize}

Our approach treats navigable-region prediction as a dense segmentation problem: the network outputs a pixel-level mask of the region to drive toward, rather than a discrete action or a bounding box around a referred object.

\subsection{Vision Encoders in Autonomous Navigation}

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are widely used for visual recognition~\cite{Krizhevsky2017ImageNet}, including ResNets~\cite{He2016Deep}, DenseNets~\cite{Huang2017Densely}, EfficientNets~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}, and VGG-style backbones~\cite{simonyan2014very}. In VLN, such encoders are often paired with language models such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks~\cite{Hochreiter1997Long} to follow instructions~\cite{anderson2018vision}. Vision Transformers (ViTs) treat an image as a sequence of patches and apply self-attention for more global context~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image,vaswani2017attention}.

Krantz et al.~\cite{krantz2020beyond}, in \textit{Beyond the Nav-Graph: Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments}, use a ResNet-50 visual encoder and argue that discrete navigation-graph assumptions can inflate reported performance. Controllable Imitative Reinforcement Learning (CIRL) similarly relies on vision encoders to learn driving policies from visual input~\cite{Liang2018CIRL}. Imaging laser radars have also been studied as complementary sensors for robotics perception~\cite{Hebert3}.

For this thesis, the key takeaway is that strong visual features---and especially features that can be aligned with language---are a prerequisite for grounding navigable regions. Chapter~\ref{ch:vln} therefore uses CLIP visual features rather than a purely vision-only CNN backbone.

\section{Basics of Natural Language Processing}

Natural Language Processing (NLP) studies how computers interpret and generate human language~\cite{ibm2023nlp,sas2023nlp}. It combines computational linguistics with statistical and neural models.

\subsection{Role of NLP in Autonomous Navigation}

In language-guided navigation, NLP is required to interpret passenger or operator commands and relate them to the visual scene. Classic work by Chen and Mooney~\cite{Chen2011Learning} showed how natural language instructions can be grounded into navigational behaviour. More recent VLN systems treat instruction following as a core multimodal problem rather than a pure control problem.

\subsection{Basic Methods in NLP}

\textbf{Language models} assign probabilities to word sequences and underpin many NLP systems~\cite{Jurafsky2023Speech,devlin2018bert}.
\textbf{Text classification} assigns documents or utterances to categories~\cite{devlin2018bert}.
\textbf{Attention mechanisms} compute importance-weighted combinations of token representations and are central to modern sequence models and transformers~\cite{Hu2019introductory,vaswani2017attention}.

For outdoor driving commands, the linguistic input is typically short (a few words to a short sentence) but referentially rich: it names objects, colours, spatial relations, and manoeuvres. The system must therefore encode word-level semantics and align them with visual features, which is exactly the role of the CLIP language encoder used in Chapter~\ref{ch:vln}.

\subsection{Text Encoders in Autonomous Navigation}

Text encoders such as word2vec, GloVe, and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) convert language into vectors usable by downstream models~\cite{towardsdatascience2023textencoding,devlin2018bert}. In VLN, Raychaudhuri et al.~\cite{Raychaudhuri2021Language} propose language-aligned supervision for off-path recovery and a metric that counts completed sub-instructions. Dual-scale graph transformers such as DUET~\cite{chen2022think} combine coarse map reasoning with fine-grained language grounding for indoor VLN.

In our setting, we prefer a jointly trained vision--language encoder (CLIP) over a language-only encoder, because the command and the road scene must be compared in a shared embedding space before dense prediction.

\section{Combining Vision and Language: Visual-Language Embeddings}

Visual--language embeddings place images and text in a common representation space so that semantic matches lie close together~\cite{Baltrusaitis2019Multimodal,Lu2019VILBERT}.

\subsection{Introduction to CLIP and Beyond}

CLIP (Contrastive Language--Image Pretraining)~\cite{radford2021learning} is trained on large-scale internet image--text pairs. An image encoder and a text encoder are jointly optimised so that matched pairs are close in a shared embedding space (Figure~\ref{fig:clip_schematic}). This dual-encoder design makes CLIP a natural backbone for VLN, where camera frames and linguistic commands must be aligned.

Larger generative models such as GPT-4~\cite{Brown2020Language} and multi-modal architectures such as XDecoder~\cite{zou2023generalized} further expand the space of vision--language systems. They are not used in this thesis, but they illustrate the broader trajectory of the field toward richer multimodal reasoning.

\subsection{Why They Matter for Language-Guided Navigation}

In language-guided navigation, visual--language embeddings let a system relate what it sees to what it is told to do~\cite{Chen2019TOUCHDOWN}. Dense-prediction adaptations of CLIP, such as converting image--text matching into pixel--text matching~\cite{Rao_Zhao_Chen_Tang_Zhu_Huang_Zhou_Lu_2022}, are particularly relevant because our outputs are segmentation masks rather than classification scores.

The practical implication for Chapters~\ref{ch:dataset} and~\ref{ch:vln} is architectural: we treat VLN as grounding a command into a navigable region using CLIP features, then hand that region to a planner, rather than translating language directly into a discrete action.

\section{Language-Guided Navigation}

\subsection{Overview}

Language-guided navigation develops systems that interpret natural language instructions and use them to move through a physical or virtual environment~\cite{anderson2018vision}. It combines NLP, computer vision, and decision-making. Challenges include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ambiguity:} the same words can mean different regions depending on context.
    \item \textbf{Variability:} different phrasings can describe the same manoeuvre.
    \item \textbf{Multimodal integration:} language, vision, and control must be combined reliably.
    \item \textbf{Real-time constraints:} decisions must keep up with a moving vehicle~\cite{anderson2018vision}.
\end{enumerate}

Applications range from passenger voice commands in autonomous vehicles, to service robots, assistive technologies, and interactive virtual environments~\cite{anderson2018vision}. For outdoor driving specifically, the hard case---and the focus of this thesis---is grounding intermediate navigable regions when the final referred goal is not yet visible.

\subsection{Existing Datasets}

Several datasets have shaped language-guided navigation research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Room-to-Room (R2R):} Anderson et al.~\cite{anderson2018vision} provide natural language instructions over Matterport3D indoor panoramas.
    \item \textbf{Touchdown:} Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2019TOUCHDOWN} target outdoor urban navigation with Google Street View and discrete actions.
    \item \textbf{StreetNav:} Mirowski et al.~\cite{Mirowski2019Learning} study instruction following on Google Street View environments.
    \item \textbf{Mapillary Street-Level Sequences (MSLS):} Warburg et al.~\cite{warburg2020mapillary} provide large-scale street-level sequences for lifelong place recognition. Mapillary Vistas~\cite{Neuhold2017mapillary} is a related semantic segmentation dataset and should not be confused with MSLS.
    \item \textbf{Active Vision Dataset (AVD):} Ammirato et al.~\cite{Ammirato2017dataset} support active vision in indoor scenes.
    \item \textbf{Stanford 2D-3D-Semantics (2D-3D-S):} Armeni et al.~\cite{armeni2017joint} provide RGB-D indoor data with semantic annotations.
    \item \textbf{AI2-THOR:} Kolve et al.~\cite{kolve2017ai2} offer interactive photorealistic indoor rooms.
    \item \textbf{House3D:} Wu et al.~\cite{wu2018building} provide large-scale diverse indoor 3D scenes for navigation and instruction following.
\end{enumerate}

Most prior datasets either assume indoor graphs, restrict outdoor agents to discrete actions on pre-recorded panoramas, or ground objects rather than navigable road regions. Few support live episode completion in a fully navigable dynamic environment. These gaps motivate CARLA-NAV.

\subsection{Existing Methods}

Language-guided navigation methods are often grouped as modular, end-to-end, or hybrid~\cite{anderson2018vision,Chen2019TOUCHDOWN}.

Modular pipelines separate language understanding, perception, and decision-making~\cite{Misra_Langford_Artzi_2017,fried2018speaker,Zhu_Zhu_Chang_Liang_2020}. Specialisation helps, but errors can propagate across stages~\cite{Codevilla_Muller_Lopez_Koltun_Dosovitskiy_2018,Misra_Langford_Artzi_2017}.

End-to-end models map instructions and images directly to actions~\cite{bojarski2016end,Codevilla_Muller_Lopez_Koltun_Dosovitskiy_2018,pomerleau1988alvinn}. They can perform well with enough data, but are harder to interpret and debug.

Hybrid approaches combine specialised modules with learned policies~\cite{Codevilla_Muller_Lopez_Koltun_Dosovitskiy_2018,bojarski2016end}. Training may use reinforcement learning, imitation learning, or both~\cite{anderson2018vision,Codevilla_Muller_Lopez_Koltun_Dosovitskiy_2018}.

Specific VLN methods include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sequence-to-sequence VLN:} Anderson et al.~\cite{anderson2018vision} map image sequences and instructions to actions with reinforcement learning from demonstrations and environment rewards.
    \item \textbf{Cross-modal matching:} Wang et al.~\cite{Wang_Huang_Celikyilmaz_Gao_Shen_Wang_Wang_Zhang_2019} propose Reinforced Cross-Modal Matching (RCM) to align sub-instructions with observed scenes.
    \item \textbf{Progress monitoring and regret:} Ma et al.~\cite{Ma_Wu_AlRegib_Xiong_Kira_2019,ma2019selfmonitoring} introduce a progress monitor, regret/backtracking, and visual--textual co-grounding so the agent can track instruction progress and decide whether to continue or reverse.
    \item \textbf{Environment Dropout (EnvDrop):} Tan et al.~\cite{tan2019learning} randomly drop visual observations during training to improve robustness to environment change.
    \item \textbf{Weakly supervised mapping:} Chen et al.~\cite{chen2022weakly} build multi-granularity maps for object localisation and goal prediction on VLN-CE.
\end{itemize}

Despite this progress, outdoor dynamic driving with continuous control and interpretable region grounding remains comparatively underexplored. That is the setting addressed in Chapters~\ref{ch:dataset} and~\ref{ch:vln}.

\section{Introduction to CARLA Simulator}

CARLA (Car Learning to Act) is an open-source simulator for autonomous driving research~\cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}. It provides urban layouts, vehicles, pedestrians, configurable weather and sensors, and an API for controlling the simulation. A server--multi-client architecture supports scalable experiments. Sensors include cameras, LiDAR, depth, and GPS. Maps follow the OpenDRIVE standard; ScenarioRunner defines traffic situations; a ROS bridge is available. CARLA also ships baseline agents and supports record/replay for controlled comparisons.

These properties make CARLA suitable for CARLA-NAV: we can spawn dynamic traffic, collect synchronized camera and pose data, project clicked navigable regions into masks, and later evaluate live navigation rather than only scoring pre-recorded frames.

\section{Summary}

This chapter established the background needed for the remainder of the thesis. Autonomous driving combines perception, planning, and control; our contribution sits at the language-conditioned perception layer. Computer vision supplies dense prediction tools; NLP and visual--language embeddings---especially CLIP---supply the alignment between commands and images. Existing VLN datasets and methods largely assume indoor graphs, discrete outdoor actions, or static grounding, as summarised in Table~\ref{table:dataset_comparison}. CARLA provides a controllable outdoor simulator in which those assumptions can be relaxed. Chapter~\ref{ch:dataset} introduces the CARLA-NAV dataset built on this foundation, and Chapter~\ref{ch:vln} presents the multi-task grounding network and live-navigation results.
