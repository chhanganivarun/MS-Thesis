\section{Introduction}

\par The ability to navigate based on linguistic descriptions of the environment is a remarkable human capability, a direct consequence of our ability to associate visual elements with their linguistic descriptions. This association is the focus of tasks such as Referring Expression Comprehension (REC) and Referring Image Segmentation (RIS), which aim to localize visual objects based on their linguistic descriptions. However, these localizations are not directly usable for navigation tasks as they do not answer the question "which region" on the road to navigate to. To address this, the task of Referring Navigable Regions (RNR) was proposed to localize the navigable regions in a static front camera image on the road corresponding to the linguistic command.

While these single image-based visual grounding methods showcase the excellent ability of neural models to correlate visual and linguistic data, they are limited in many ways. They are trained on carefully paired data, assuming that the region to be grounded is always visible in the frame. They give an erroneous output when the region to be grounded is not currently visible, is occluded, or goes out of frame (as the carrier moves). Such scenarios are part of everyday language-guided navigation; for instance, consider a command “Take a right once you see the traffic signal" the traffic signal here may not be immediately visible.

Another major limitation is that single image-based predictions are devoid of any temporal context (short term or long term), which is crucial in successful navigation, especially in a dynamically changing environment. Finally, since single image methods are evaluated on frames from pre-recorded videos, they cannot be validated appropriately on their ability to complete the entire episode (from start to desired finish).

Most existing works on language-guided navigation, both in indoor and outdoor environments, make certain assumptions that limit their applicability in real-world scenarios. For instance, many indoor navigation methods assume that the navigational environment is fully known, allowing them to discretize the known map into a graphical representation. However, such an approach is not practical for outdoor settings where the environment is unknown and constantly changing. Moreover, even if the environment is known, discretizing the maps is not feasible when more refined localization and maneuvers are required, such as "stop beside the person with a red cap".

Existing methods for both indoor and outdoor Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) often model navigation as a selection from a set of discrete actions or as a reinforcement learning problem. For example, the commonly used Touchdown dataset consists of pre-recorded Google Street View images and allows navigation across street views by choosing from a set of four discrete actions, i.e., FORWARD, RIGHT, LEFT, and STOP. This discretization of the action space and the environment limits the type of navigational maneuvers that can be performed. For instance, these methods cannot be used for commands like "park between the two cars on the right", which require fine-grained control.

These issues become particularly apparent in a dynamically changing environment, where fine-grained control of the car’s navigation and a fully navigable environment is required to adapt to the dynamic surroundings and perform navigational maneuvers based on the linguistic command.

To address these limitations, we decided to create a new dataset for autonomous navigation. The dataset was created using the CARLA simulator, an open-source simulator for autonomous driving research. In the following sections, we will discuss our initial attempts at data collection using the video game Grand Theft Auto V (GTA V), the challenges we faced, and how we overcame these challenges to create a robust dataset for autonomous navigation research. Our dataset, combined with a visual grounding-based approach, allows for fine-grained control over the vehicle as it enables navigation to any drivable region on the road. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt towards live language-based navigation in outdoor environments.

\section{Initial Data Collection with GTA V} 
 
In the section "Initial Data Collection with GTA V", we will discuss our initial attempts to collect data using the Grand Theft Auto V (GTA V) video game and the DeepGTAV and V-Pilot mods. The game’s realistic graphics, diverse urban environments, and dynamic traffic scenarios made it an appealing choice for simulating real-world driving conditions. We used DeepGTAV, a plugin for GTA V that allows the extraction of a wide range of data from the game, including images from the game’s camera, the position and speed of the player’s vehicle, and information about other vehicles and pedestrians in the game.

Our initial approach was to use a deep learning model to predict three outputs: steering, acceleration, and brake values. However, we encountered several challenges with this approach. Firstly, the deep learning model did not have sufficient information about the rules of the game or a comprehensive understanding of what it was supposed to do. Predicting just three numbers and trying to extract an understanding from them proved to be difficult. This is in line with the findings of Bansal et al., 2018, who noted that deep learning models often struggle with understanding complex tasks without sufficient context or guidance. This issue is further highlighted in the work of Dosovitskiy et al., 2017, who found that the lack of high-level understanding in deep learning models often leads to unpredictable and erratic behavior.

Secondly, we found that we could not use supervised learning without also using reinforcement learning. However, we decided against using reinforcement learning for two reasons. Firstly, GTA V does not allow for the collection of sufficient data to feed a reward function, which is a common challenge in reinforcement learning as noted by Sutton and Barto, 2018. Secondly, defining a reward function for driving based on language commands is a complex task, as it requires a detailed understanding of both the driving environment and the nuances of language. This is a well-known challenge in the field of reinforcement learning, as highlighted by Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017.

Given these challenges, we decided to switch to a different approach: using supervised learning to predict segmentation masks. This approach has several advantages. Firstly, it allows for a more detailed and nuanced understanding of the driving environment, as it involves predicting a segmentation mask for each frame rather than just three values. This is supported by the work of Hanselmann et al., 2021, who found that segmentation-based approaches can handle complex tasks more effectively than proposal-based approaches.

Secondly, supervised learning allows for more precise and consistent control over the vehicle's movements, as it involves predicting a detailed segmentation mask rather than just three values. This is in line with the findings of Cao et al., 2019, who noted that supervised learning methods can achieve high performance even with few training examples.

Finally, using supervised learning to predict segmentation masks allows for a more interpretable model, as the segmentation masks provide a clear visual representation of the model's predictions. This is supported by the work of Uřičář et al., 2021, who found that models trained on refined annotations perform significantly better than those trained on noisy annotations.

In the next section, we will discuss our decision to switch to the CARLA simulator and our process for collecting data using this platform.

\section{Switching to CARLA for Data Collection}

After encountering several limitations with our initial approach using GTA V, we decided to switch to a different simulation environment for our data collection: CARLA. CARLA, an open-source simulator specifically designed for autonomous driving research, offers several advantages over GTA V that made it a more suitable choice for our purposes.

Firstly, CARLA is designed with research in mind. It provides a high level of control and flexibility over the simulation environment, allowing us to easily adjust various parameters and conditions to suit our needs. This includes control over the weather, time of day, and the behavior of other vehicles and pedestrians in the environment. This level of control is crucial for creating a diverse and robust dataset for autonomous navigation.

Secondly, CARLA provides accurate and consistent physics simulation, ensuring that the vehicle's movements and interactions with the environment are realistic and reliable. This is a significant improvement over the erratic and unpredictable outputs we encountered with GTA V.

Thirdly, CARLA provides access to detailed information about the environment, including the 3D positions of objects and the layout of the road network. This information is essential for our task of predicting navigable regions based on linguistic commands.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, CARLA allows us to shift our approach from predicting steering, acceleration, and brake values to predicting a segmentation map for navigation. This approach, known as visual grounding, involves associating the linguistic description of navigable regions with their visual counterparts in the environment. By predicting a segmentation map, our model can provide a visual representation of the navigable regions corresponding to the linguistic command, making the predictions more interpretable and easier to validate. This shift would not have been feasible in GTA V due to the game's inherent limitations. GTA V, being a commercial video game, is not designed to provide the level of access to the game's internal data that would be required for such a task. While mods like DeepGTAV and V-Pilot can provide some additional data, they do not provide the detailed, per-pixel information about the environment that is necessary for predicting a segmentation map. Furthermore, GTA V does not provide access to live camera intrinsic and orientation matrix, which are crucial for transforming 2D screen coordinates into 3D world coordinates. This makes it difficult to accurately associate the 2D segmentation map with the 3D world, limiting the effectiveness of the visual grounding approach in this environment. In contrast, CARLA provides detailed, per-pixel information about the environment, including the 3D world coordinates of objects. This makes it possible to accurately predict a segmentation map and associate it with the corresponding navigable regions in the 3D world, overcoming the limitations of GTA V and enabling a more effective approach to language-guided navigation.

In summary, the switch to CARLA allowed us to overcome the challenges we faced with GTA V and adopt a more effective approach for our task of language-guided navigation. The following sections will discuss our methodology for data collection in CARLA and the structure of our new dataset.

\section{Data Collection Methodology}

The data collection process in CARLA was a collaborative effort involving three roles: the observer, the navigator, and the verifier. Each role had specific responsibilities and contributed to the overall quality and accuracy of the data collected.

The observer's role was to provide the linguistic commands for the navigator to follow. The observer was responsible for creating clear and unambiguous commands that would guide the navigator through the CARLA environment. The observer was not directly involved in the driving or annotation process, but their linguistic commands formed the basis for the navigation and subsequent data annotation.

The navigator's role was to drive the vehicle in the CARLA environment, following the linguistic commands provided by the observer. The navigator was also responsible for annotating the navigable regions corresponding to the observer's commands. This was done by clicking on the 2D screen coordinates of the navigable regions in the front camera view. The navigator was instructed to drive as naturally as possible, mimicking real-world driving behavior, and to annotate the navigable regions as accurately as possible.

The verifier's role was to check the quality of the annotations provided by the navigator and the appropriateness of the navigation with respect to the linguistic command given by the observer. The verifier would review the 2D screen coordinates clicked by the navigator and confirm whether they accurately represented the navigable regions described in the linguistic command. If the verifier found any inaccuracies or if the navigation did not correctly correspond to the given command, they would reject the episode from being included in the dataset.

The 2D screen coordinates clicked by the navigator were then transformed into 3D world coordinates using the camera intrinsic and orientation matrix provided by CARLA. This transformation allowed us to map the 2D navigable regions annotated on the screen to their corresponding regions in the 3D world.

Once we had the 3D world coordinates of the navigable regions, we used them to generate segmentation masks. A segmentation mask is a binary image that indicates which pixels in the image belong to the navigable region. Each pixel in the segmentation mask corresponds to a pixel in the front camera view. If the pixel is part of the navigable region, it is marked as 1 in the segmentation mask; otherwise, it is marked as 0.

In addition to generating segmentation masks, the 3D world coordinates of the navigable regions were also used to set the target destination for each episode. The target destination was defined as the final navigable region indicated by the observer's linguistic command. This was typically the last region clicked by the navigator during the annotation process.

The target destination was saved as part of the dataset for each episode. This provided a clear goal for the navigation task and allowed us to evaluate the performance of navigation models not just on their ability to follow the linguistic commands and navigate the annotated regions, but also on their ability to reach the intended destination.

This additional layer of complexity - having a defined target destination - made our dataset more challenging and realistic. In real-world navigation tasks, the goal is not just to follow a set of instructions, but to reach a specific destination. By including this aspect in our dataset, we made it a more effective tool for training and evaluating language-guided navigation models.

This data collection methodology allowed us to collect a rich dataset of linguistic commands, navigable regions, and corresponding segmentation masks. The dataset provides a valuable resource for training and evaluating models for language-guided navigation.


\section{Dataset Creation}

The creation of the CARLA-NAV dataset was a meticulous process involving several steps. The process was divided into three main roles: the observer, the navigator, and the verifier. Each role had a specific task to perform, and the roles were rotated among the team members to avoid any potential bias.

The observer's role was to provide a language command for navigation. This command was based on the observer's understanding of the environment and the desired navigation path. The language command was not scripted or pre-determined, but was generated on-the-fly during the data collection process. This approach allowed us to capture a wide range of natural language commands, reflecting the diversity and complexity of real-world navigation instructions.

The navigator's role was to navigate the CARLA environment based on the observer's language command. The navigation was performed through mouse clicks on the navigable regions in the environment. Each mouse click was transformed into 3D world coordinates, which were then used to generate a segmentation mask for the navigable region. The navigator's task was challenging, as it required a precise understanding of the observer's command and a careful selection of the navigable regions. The navigator was provided with a "restart" option to restart the navigation in case of erroneous clicks. For ambiguous commands, the navigator did what they felt at that time was right or most obvious to them. This approach, however, could induce bias, and we observed that it differed across our different navigators.

The verifier's role was to verify the recorded episode for inclusion in the dataset. The verifier reviewed the observer's language command, the navigator's path, and the generated segmentation masks, and decided whether the episode correctly represented the given command. This verification process was crucial to ensure the quality and accuracy of the dataset.

The 2D point corresponding to the mouse click in the front view of the car was transformed into the 3D world coordinates using Inverse Projective transform. This 3D position was passed as input to the local planner to navigate the CARLA environment. We used CARLA’s default planner for our case; however, owing to the modular design, in future it can be replaced by any state-of-the-art planner.

An episode comprised of multiple mouse clicks, until the final navigable region was not visible in the front view. These intermediate mouse clicks signified the intermediate navigable regions, and the last mouse click depicted the final goal region corresponding to the command. The mouse clicks were converted into segmentation masks by drawing a 4m x 4m rectangle (approx size of a car) in the top view, centered at the mouse click. The rectangle was then projected in the front-camera. Overall, only the language commands and mouse clicks required manual effort, the rest of the annotation process was fully automated.

Despite our best efforts to maintain a rigorous and unbiased data collection process, we encountered several challenges. One of the main challenges was the inherent subjectivity in interpreting and executing the language commands. Different observers and navigators might interpret the same command in slightly different ways, leading to variations in the navigated paths and segmentation masks. To mitigate this issue, we implemented a rotation system for the roles, so that the dataset would not be overly influenced by any single individual's interpretation.

Another challenge was the potential bias in the language commands. The observers, being humans, might unconsciously favor certain types of commands or use certain phrases more frequently. This could potentially introduce a bias in the dataset, skewing the distribution of commands. To address this, we encouraged the observers to vary their language use and to think creatively about different ways to describe the navigation tasks.

Despite these challenges, we believe that the CARLA-NAV dataset represents a significant step forward in the field of language-guided navigation. Its creation involved a careful and thoughtful process, and we hope that it will serve as a valuable resource for researchers and practitioners in the field.

\section{Dataset Characteristics}

The CARLA-NAV dataset, which we have curated, is a comprehensive collection of episodic data, each episode consisting of a language command and the corresponding video from the CARLA Simulator of navigation towards the final goal region described by the command. The ground truth segmentation mask for each frame either corresponds to the final or an intermediate navigable region. Each frame is additionally annotated with a plausible future trajectory of the vehicle in the next few frames.

The dataset includes video sequences captured in 8 different maps, 14 distinct weather conditions, and a diverse range of dynamically moving vehicles and pedestrians in the environment. The language commands in our dataset contain detailed visual descriptions of the environment and describe a wide range of maneuvers. Commands can either have a single maneuver (e.g., park behind the black car on the left) or multiple maneuvers (e.g., take a right turn and park near the bus stand). A maximum of three maneuvers are included in the dataset.

The dataset is divided into three splits: training, validation, and testing. The training split consists of 500 episodes, totaling 75,010 frames, with an average command length of 6.92 words and an average of 1.94 clicks per command. The validation split consists of 25 episodes, totaling 3,300 frames, with an average command length of 6.76 words and an average of 2.00 clicks per command. The testing split consists of 34 episodes, totaling 4,987 frames, with an average command length of 7.44 words and an average of 1.97 clicks per command.

The commands in the dataset mainly consist of up to three subcommands, most having two subcommands. These subcommands were mainly of types turning, going straight ahead, stopping, and parking. The subcommands referenced different types of objects like pedestrians (mainly for stopping near a pedestrian in a specific attire), infrastructure (like a flag pole, its color, etc.; or a building its type, its color, parts like shade or something sticking out; or other elements like bus top, post box or garbage bin etc.), and road elements like traffic lights, crossing, road sign etc.; other moving cars; stationary cars and scenery like some bush etc.

In conclusion, the CARLA-NAV dataset provides a rich and diverse set of data for training and testing autonomous navigation models. Its comprehensive annotations and diverse set of scenarios make it a valuable resource for researchers in the field of autonomous driving.

\section{Data Collection Challenges}
In the process of data collection for autonomous driving research, we encountered several challenges. One of the most significant issues was related to asynchronous programming in CARLA. CARLA, being a real-time simulator, operates in an asynchronous manner. This means that the simulator does not wait for our code to finish executing before moving on to the next frame. This led to a discrepancy between the frames we were processing and the frames being displayed by the simulator. To address this, we had to carefully manage the synchronization between our code and the simulator, ensuring that we were always working with the correct frames.

Another challenge we faced was related to the handling of slopes in the environment. The transformation of 2D screen coordinates into 3D world coordinates is a crucial part of our data collection process. However, this transformation becomes complex and error-prone when dealing with slopes. The issue arises because the 2D to 3D projection assumes a flat ground plane, which is not the case when the vehicle is on a slope. This issue was particularly pronounced when the vehicle transitioned from an upward slope to a flat surface. The sudden change in the ground plane led to inaccuracies in the 3D world coordinates, which in turn affected the quality of our data. We had to devise strategies to handle these situations and ensure the accuracy of our data.

Finally, we faced the challenge of repetition in the train, validation, and test data. Given the finite nature of the CARLA environment, there was a risk of having the same or similar scenarios appearing in different splits of the dataset. This could potentially lead to overfitting, as the model might simply memorize specific scenarios instead of learning to generalize. To mitigate this, we ensured a thorough shuffling and randomization of the data during the splitting process. We also made sure to spawn the vehicle at different locations in the environment for each episode, adding to the diversity of the scenarios in the dataset.

Despite these challenges, we were able to successfully collect a robust dataset for autonomous navigation research. The issues we encountered and the solutions we devised have not only enriched our understanding of the complexities involved in autonomous driving research but also provided valuable insights that could guide future data collection efforts in this field.
